How one lesson works, step by step

Every lesson in the course runs this loop. Right now: How fast is the car?.

1
Make practice examples

The simulator generates ~300 examples with answers (e.g. 'this waterfall has a leak at channel 14').

▼
2
Make a quiz and hide the answers

300 fresh examples it has never seen. Answers are kept from the student.

▼
3
Study

It turns each signal into numbers (features), then fits a model. It starts with the crudest way of looking (raw statistics) and the simplest model (a straight line), on purpose.

▼
4
Take the quiz and grade itself

Accuracy, precision, recall. Every wrong answer is saved, with the signal itself.

▼
5
PASS? → final exam

If it beats the pass mark, it sits a final exam on a third set it has never seen. That score is the honest one.

▼
6
FAIL? → work out WHY

Can't even get practice right = it's looking at the wrong things (underfit). Practice good, quiz bad = it memorised (overfit). Close = it needs precision.

▼
7
…and WHERE

It slices the quiz by condition (noise level, wind, speed, porosity…) and finds where it fails most, e.g. 'fails 62% when SNR is below −12 dB'.

▼
8
Pick a fix

Underfit → unlock a new way of looking (spectrum, spatial, moveout…) or a smarter model. Overfit → more examples. Weak spot → extra practice in exactly that condition.

▼
9
Re-study its mistakes

Every quiz question it got wrong goes back into practice with 2–3× weight. Then back to step 1.

▼
10
Give up after 8 tries

Marked STUCK and logged, so you can see it. The course moves on.

Leak module: what it's looking at

A fiber-optic cable buried along a pipe acts like thousands of microphones. A waterfall plot shows every spot on the cable (rows) over time (across). Dark = shaking.

1
Traffic

A diagonal streak. The car moves along the cable, so its slope = its speed.

▼
2
Leak

A thin, steady horizontal line in one spot: a narrow-band hiss that never stops.

▼
3
Digging

Short, sharp bursts in one place.

▼
4
Wind

Gentle low-frequency shaking all along the cable at once.

▼
5
How it tells them apart

Spectrum (which pitches?), spatial (where?), stationarity (steady or bursty?), moveout (does it travel?), local spectrum (is one spot humming?).

Fluid module: what it's looking at

Rock is a sponge of mineral grains with pores. The pores hold brine, oil, gas or air, and each changes how sound travels.

1
P-waves (push-pull)

Travel through rock AND fluid. They slow down a lot in gas.

▼
2
S-waves (side-to-side)

Can't travel through liquid. Sensors above a fluid pocket see an S-wave 'shadow'.

▼
3
Gassmann's equation

Predicts how much faster the rock gets when you swap gas for brine in the pores.

▼
4
AVO

How strongly a layer reflects at wider angles. Gas sands get dimmer or brighter in a telltale way.

▼
5
Attenuation (Q)

Fluids soak up high frequencies, so the pulse arrives duller.

▼
6
Real answer key

Wells drilled into the field measured what's really there. Those depths are the labels.

Real-data loop and the honest score

1
Check size before downloading

HTTP/S3 size check. ≤ 5 GB: download (resumes if cut off). > 5 GB: stop and ask you.

▼
2
Build the answer key

Fluid: well-log interpretation (water saturation) → water/hydrocarbon at each depth. DAS: published event catalogues → 'event' / 'no event' for each time window.

▼
3
Lock away whole wells / time blocks

Saved to disk once. Never trained on, never used to pick settings.

▼
4
Warm up

A few synthetic examples per class help it start; they count half as much as real ones.

▼
5
Rounds

Different wells play 'quiz' each round. Mistakes get double weight next round; if it plateaus it tries a smarter model.

▼
6
Stop

After 3 rounds with no improvement, or 10 rounds.

▼
7
Score the locked set once

That number goes on the dashboard as the honest accuracy.
